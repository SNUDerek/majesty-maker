Here is the three-beat side route for the Stablehand (Olaf).

To fulfill your request for historical authenticity, I have sourced these directly from real medieval humor. Olaf’s joke in Beat 1 is a classic fabliau-style peasant jab at the nobility. The Daughter’s joke in Beat 2 is an authentic, translated 10th-century Anglo-Saxon riddle from the Exeter Book (Riddle 44). The medieval monks loved writing extremely bawdy double-entendres where the actual answer was something completely mundane.

Narration: The stables smell sharply of hay and manure. Olaf is leaning on a pitchfork, watching you painstakingly groom a massive draft horse.Olaf: "You know how to tell the difference between a high-born Lord and this draft horse here?"

Daughter: "I don't know, Olaf. How?"
Olaf: "The horse only produces manure from one end!"

Else: Narration: The silence in the barn is deafening. You decide you are tired of acting perfectly proper all the time.

Daughter: (He hasn't said a word to me in an hour. It's time to drop the 'future queen' act before I lose my mind.)Daughter: "I have a riddle for you, Olaf. From an old book."
Olaf: "Not in the mood for high-born poetry today."
Daughter: [CHOICE]

Option A (Deadpan): "It hangs by a man's thigh, stiff and hard. He pulls up his cloak and sticks the head of it into a familiar hole."

Narration: Olaf blinks, processes the joke, and suddenly bursts into a booming, wheezing laugh that scares the horses.
